4. A Practical System for Chess Strategy

Strategy is the art of improving the position and creating favorable conditions. A plan should be based on the position—not on a favorite idea you want to play regardless of circumstances.

Step 1: Evaluate the position

Use a short scan:

  • Material: Who is ahead, and are there compensation factors?
  • Kings: Is either king exposed or short of defenders?
  • Pieces: Which side is more active? Which piece is worst?
  • Structure: What pawns are weak, isolated, doubled, backward, or passed?
  • Space and squares: Who controls useful territory and outposts?
  • Initiative: Who is making threats and forcing the other side to respond?

Step 2: Identify the target

Targets include weak pawns, weak squares, an exposed king, an unprotected piece, or a piece with no good squares. A weakness becomes strategically useful when you can attack it, occupy a square around it, or make the opponent tie pieces down to defense.

Step 3: Restrict counterplay

Ask what freeing move the opponent wants. Can you prevent a pawn break, contest an open file, exchange an active attacker, or deny a key square? Restriction is not passive defense: it reduces the opponent's useful choices while improving your own position.

Step 4: Improve and coordinate

Place rooks on useful files, knights on stable outposts, bishops on active diagonals, and the queen where it supports the plan without becoming a target. Coordinate pieces so they attack the same weakness or support a pawn break.

Step 5: Choose the right pawn break

Pawn breaks open lines and change the structure. Before playing one, ask:

  • Will it open a file or diagonal for my pieces?
  • Will it create a weakness in the opponent's camp?
  • What squares become weak after my pawn moves?
  • Will the opponent gain activity or a passed pawn?

A useful rule from our earlier notes: first improve the placement of your pieces; then improve your pawns. If no useful piece improvement is available, look for the right pawn break.

Step 6: Convert the advantage

When you have an advantage, avoid drifting. Improve the worst piece, add a second target, exchange the opponent's active pieces when favorable, and simplify only when the resulting position remains favorable. Do not trade merely because you are ahead—trade to make the win easier.
