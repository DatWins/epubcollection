1. How to Think During a Chess Game

A useful thought process is not about calculating every possible move. It is about asking the right questions in the right order.

The practical thinking loop

  1. Safety first. What is attacked? Are there checks, captures, or direct threats for either side?
  2. Understand the position. Compare king safety, material, piece activity, pawn structure, space, and weaknesses.
  3. Ask what the opponent wants. Identify their threat, ideal square, pawn break, or plan. Can you prevent or reduce it?
  4. Find candidate moves. Consider a few serious options: improve a piece, restrict the opponent, create a threat, make a pawn break, or calculate a forcing line.
  5. Calculate forcing moves first. Examine checks, captures, and threats. Look at the opponent's strongest reply—not just the reply you hope for.
  6. Compare the resulting positions. Which move improves your position, keeps your king safe, and leaves you with a clear next step?
  7. Blunder-check before moving. After your intended move, what can the opponent check, capture, or threaten?
When no tactic or urgent threat is present: improve your worst-placed piece. A useful positional move often makes several future moves easier.

Candidate moves: breadth before depth

Do not calculate one attractive move endlessly while ignoring alternatives. First identify a small set of plausible candidates. Then calculate the most forcing and relevant lines. The goal is not to see everything; it is to compare the moves that matter.

A compact over-the-board checklist

  • What changed after the opponent's last move?
  • What is threatened?
  • What is my opponent's plan?
  • What is my worst piece, and where should it go?
  • Is there a forcing move or a useful pawn break?
  • What is the opponent's best reply to my choice?
