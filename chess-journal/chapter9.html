9. Kasparov’s Rules for Attacking

An attack succeeds when activity, coordination, open lines, and calculation work together. These practical rules summarize the attacking principles from our earlier Kasparov lesson.

  1. Understand the opening. Know the purpose of your development and pawn moves. A sound opening should help your pieces become active and prepare a plan.
  2. Open lines before attacking. Attacks need routes. Open files, diagonals, or central lines when doing so helps your pieces reach the target.
  3. Bring every piece into the attack. Count the attackers and defenders. An attack is often premature if several of your pieces cannot participate.
  4. Keep creating threats. Useful threats force the opponent to spend time defending and can keep the initiative on your side.
  5. Improve quietly when there is no breakthrough. If a direct attack is not ready, centralize, coordinate, and improve the placement of your pieces instead of forcing matters.
  6. Attack where you have the advantage in numbers. Look for a target where your attacking pieces outnumber its defenders. Avoid launching an attack against a well-protected point without a concrete reason.
  7. Combine creativity with calculation. A surprising idea is valuable only if it works. Check the opponent’s strongest defensive resources and tactical counterattacks.
  8. Evaluate as well as calculate. Calculation answers what happens in a line; evaluation tells you whether the resulting position is good for you. Use both.
  9. Study your own games. Review missed attacks, premature sacrifices, uncoordinated pieces, and moments when a quiet improving move was stronger than a direct assault.
Attacking framework: UNDERSTAND → DEVELOP → OPEN → COORDINATE → PRESSURE → IMPROVE → OUTNUMBER → CALCULATE → EVALUATE.

Before you launch an attack

  • Is the opponent’s king or another target genuinely vulnerable?
  • Can I open lines that favor my pieces?
  • How many attackers can join, and how many defenders are present?
  • Are my own king and back rank safe?
  • What is the opponent’s strongest defensive move?
  • If the attack does not win immediately, will I still have a good position?

Key lesson: Do not attack simply because you want to attack. Improve the position until the attack is justified, then calculate concretely.
