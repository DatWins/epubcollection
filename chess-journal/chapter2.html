2. Capablanca-Inspired Positional Rules

These 13 practical rules synthesize the Capablanca-style principles and positional lessons collected in our earlier chess notes. They are a training framework, not a claim that Capablanca wrote this exact numbered list.

  1. Develop your pieces. Bring your minor pieces into the game instead of repeatedly moving the same piece without a concrete reason.
  2. Fight for the center. Central control gives pieces more routes and makes it harder for the opponent to operate freely.
  3. Castle and respect king safety. Complete development while keeping your king out of unnecessary danger.
  4. Coordinate before attacking. An attack is stronger when the pieces support one another and can join quickly.
  5. Improve your worst piece. Find the least useful piece and give it a better square, file, diagonal, or target.
  6. Ask what the opponent wants. Spot their plan early. Prevention can be more effective than reacting after it succeeds.
  7. Restrict counterplay. Limit useful pawn breaks, active squares, open files, and tactical opportunities for the other side.
  8. Create and target weaknesses. A weakness matters when you can attack it, occupy a square near it, or force the opponent to defend passively.
  9. Use pawn moves purposefully. Pawns cannot move backward. Before advancing one, consider the squares and lines you leave behind.
  10. Keep the initiative when it is real. Active threats can be worth more than a small material gain, but do not confuse activity with a sound attack.
  11. Trade with a reason. Exchanges can remove a defender, reduce counterplay, reach a favorable endgame, or relieve pressure. Avoid automatic trades.
  12. Protect lasting advantages. Extra material, a healthier pawn structure, a strong outpost, or a weak enemy pawn can remain useful long after a temporary attack fades.
  13. Convert simply and accurately. When ahead, reduce the opponent's activity, avoid unnecessary complications, and choose a favorable endgame when appropriate.

The guiding question

“What does my opponent want, and what is my most useful improving move?” This question connects strategic awareness with practical action.
